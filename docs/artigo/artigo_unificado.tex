\begin{abstract}
Low-bitwidth floating-point formats such as IEEE 754 half-precision (\texttt{FP16}) and Open Compute Project 8-bit formats (\texttt{OFP8 E4M3} and \texttt{E5M2}) are widely adopted to accelerate machine learning and scientific workloads. However, their narrow dynamic range makes them highly susceptible to numerical overflow and underflow when processing unscaled sparse matrices or wide-ranging gradients. Recently, Hunhold proposed \emph{Takum}, a tapered-precision number system that guarantees a vast dynamic range of $2^{\pm 255} \approx 10^{\pm 77}$ for bitwidths $N \ge 12$ (\texttt{T16} and \texttt{T32}) and $\approx 10^{\pm 72}$ in 8-bit precision (\texttt{T8}), while concentrating precision near unity. In this paper, we present the design, implementation, and numerical evaluation of \texttt{Zvtakum}, a custom RISC-V Vector (\texttt{RVV}) instruction set extension supporting linear \texttt{T8}, \texttt{T16}, and \texttt{T32} formats. We design a unified 5-bit header decoder (\texttt{S, D, R}) validated via numerical quadrature, implement core arithmetic and extended-precision dot-product instructions (\texttt{vtadd.vv}, \texttt{vtsub.vv}, \texttt{vtmul.vv}, \texttt{vtdot.vv}, and \texttt{vncvt.t.t}) in the \texttt{custom-1} opcode space inside the Spike simulator, and evaluate them on Sparse Matrix-Vector Multiplication (SpMV) and dense General Matrix-Vector Multiplication (GEMV) workloads against a 128-bit quadruple-precision oracle (\texttt{\_\_float128}). Experimental results show that \texttt{Zvtakum} completely avoids the $100\%$ overflow/underflow failure exhibited by \texttt{OFP8} and \texttt{FP16} under wide dynamic ranges, while \texttt{vtdot.vv} reduces median relative error by up to $2.7\times$ in strip-mined execution ($3\times$ to $7\times$ in full-row accumulation) and removes architectural dependencies on the \texttt{frm} and \texttt{fflags} CSRs.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The growing computational demands of deep neural networks and large-scale scientific simulations have driven a decisive shift toward reduced-precision arithmetic. Moving from 32-bit single-precision (\texttt{FP32}) to 16-bit (\texttt{FP16}) and 8-bit (\texttt{OFP8 E4M3} and \texttt{E5M2})~\cite{ocp_fp8} representations proportionally reduces memory bandwidth bottlenecks and increases vector throughput. Nevertheless, conventional IEEE 754 floating-point formats~\cite{ieee754} allocate a fixed number of bits to the exponent field, creating a rigid trade-off between dynamic range and significand precision. In 8-bit formats such as \texttt{OFP8 E4M3} (maximum normal value $448$) and 16-bit \texttt{FP16} (maximum normal value $65504$), computations involving wide dynamic ranges frequently suffer catastrophic overflow or underflow.

To overcome these limitations, tapered-precision formats such as Posit~\cite{gustafson2017posit} and, more recently, \emph{Takum}~\cite{hunhold2024takum} dynamically vary the length of the exponent encoding. Proposed by Hunhold in 2024, linear Takum bounds the characteristic field to at most 7 bits, achieving a dynamic range of $2^{\pm 255} \approx 10^{\pm 77}$ that remains constant for all bitwidths $N \ge 12$ (and reaches $\approx 8.8 \times 10^{71} \approx 10^{\pm 72}$ in \texttt{T8} due to 3-bit characteristic truncation). Consequently, even an 8-bit Takum (\texttt{T8}) achieves a vastly wider dynamic range than IEEE \texttt{FP32} ($\approx 10^{\pm 38}$) while retaining a shared 5-bit prefix structure (\texttt{S, D, R}) that simplifies hardware decoding across \texttt{T8}, \texttt{T16}, and \texttt{T32}~\cite{hunhold2025avx}.

In this work, we bridge Takum arithmetic and vector processing by proposing \texttt{Zvtakum}, a RISC-V vector extension for linear Takum arithmetic. The main contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A unified C++ arithmetic core and 5-bit prefix decoder for \texttt{T8}, \texttt{T16}, and \texttt{T32} validated bit-by-bit against \texttt{libtakum} and composite numerical quadrature rules;
    \item The specification and functional integration of the \texttt{Zvtakum} ISA extension within the RISC-V \texttt{custom-1} opcode space inside the Spike simulator, including an extended-precision vector dot product (\texttt{vtdot.vv});
    \item A comprehensive numerical and architectural evaluation across CSR SpMV and dense ML GEMV workloads against an IEEE 754 quadruple-precision (\texttt{\_\_float128}) oracle, disentangling format and accumulator gains alongside an assembly instruction count and CSR dependency analysis.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{IEEE 754 and OCP FP8 Representations}
In IEEE 754 floating-point arithmetic~\cite{ieee754}, a real number is encoded using a sign bit $S$, a fixed-width biased exponent $E$, and a significand fraction $M$. Shrinking the word size to 16 bits (\texttt{FP16}, 5 exponent bits) or 8 bits severely restricts the exponent range. The Open Compute Project (OCP) defines two 8-bit variants~\cite{ocp_fp8}: \texttt{E4M3} (4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits, saturating at $\pm 448$) and \texttt{E5M2} (5 exponent bits, 2 mantissa bits, range $\pm 57344$). Both formats exhibit high quantization noise and rapid saturation when matrix entries span multiple orders of magnitude.

\subsection{The Linear Takum Format}
Takum arithmetic~\cite{hunhold2024takum} replaces fixed-width exponents with a 3-bit regime field $R$ combined with a direction bit $D$ and sign bit $S$. For an $n$-bit linear Takum (\texttt{T8}, \texttt{T16}, or \texttt{T32}), the first 5 bits $(S, D, R)$ completely determine the bitwidth $r \in [0, 7]$ of the characteristic field $C$:
\begin{equation}
    r = \begin{cases} R, & \text{if } D = 1 \\ 7 - R, & \text{if } D = 0 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The signed characteristic $c$ and the remaining $p = \max(0, n - 5 - r)$ mantissa bits $M$ yield a linear floating-point value $(-1)^S \cdot 2^c \cdot (1 + M / 2^p)$ for positive values ($S=0$) and its monotonic negation for $S=1$. Because $r \le 7$, any bitwidth $N \ge 12$ accommodates the full 5-bit header plus 7 characteristic bits, providing a constant dynamic range of $2^{\pm 255} \approx 10^{\pm 77}$ (while \texttt{T8} truncates characteristics with $r > 3$, spanning $\approx 10^{\pm 72}$).

\subsection{RISC-V Vector Extension (RVV)}
The RISC-V Vector Extension (\texttt{RVV 1.0})~\cite{riscv_v_spec} employs a vector-length agnostic programming model governed by \texttt{vtype} (which configures the Selected Element Width \texttt{SEW} and register grouping \texttt{LMUL}) and \texttt{vl} (active vector length). Application loops use \texttt{vsetvli} to strip-mine arbitrary-length vectors across hardware vector register widths (\texttt{VLEN}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Gustafson and Yonemoto~\cite{gustafson2017posit} introduced Posit arithmetic as a tapered-precision drop-in replacement for IEEE 754 floating-point numbers. While Posits offer higher accuracy near unity than IEEE floats of equal width, their variable-length unary regime can occupy the entire $n$-bit word. Consequently, Posit dynamic range varies drastically with bitwidth (remaining overly narrow at 8 bits), and hardware decoders must serialize regime leading-zero/one detection across all $n - 1$ bits before extracting the exponent and fraction.

To address these hardware bottlenecks, Hunhold~\cite{hunhold2024takum} proposed Takum arithmetic in 2024, bounding the regime prefix to exactly 3 bits ($R$) plus a direction bit ($D$). Evaluations on linear systems and matrix workloads demonstrated that linear Takums match or exceed Posit accuracy while preventing overflow in 8- and 16-bit formats. Subsequently, Hunhold~\cite{hunhold2025avx} investigated SIMD ISA extensions for Takum arithmetic on fixed-width packed-SIMD architectures (Intel AVX-512 and Arm SVE), highlighting how the invariant 5-bit prefix $(S, D, R)$ enables a shared decoder front-end across 8-, 16-, 32-, and 64-bit lanes. Unlike packed SIMD extensions, our work integrates linear Takum arithmetic into the stateful, vector-length agnostic RISC-V Vector (\texttt{RVV}) architecture (\texttt{Zvtakum}), introducing a fused extended-precision reduction instruction (\texttt{vtdot.vv}) and evaluating strip-mined SpMV and GEMV kernels inside the Spike ISA simulator.

\section{Proposed Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

We implemented the Takum datapath as a bit-exact C++ functional model and integrated
it into the Spike RISC-V ISA simulator as the \emph{Zvtakum} vector extension. The
simulator was chosen over an RTL description because it allowed us to validate
millions of operations against a reference library and to reuse the \emph{same} source
file inside the ISA simulator, so no second copy of the arithmetic exists.

\subsection{Format and Decoder}
We use the linear takum of Hunhold~\cite{hunhold2024takum} with $n\in\{8,16,32\}$ bits:
\begin{equation}
\underbrace{S}_{1}\,\underbrace{D}_{1}\,\underbrace{R}_{3}\,\underbrace{C}_{r}\,\underbrace{M}_{p},
\qquad r = D\,?\,R : 7-R,\quad p = n-5-r .
\end{equation}
The regime field has a fixed width of three bits, so the number of characteristic
bits $r$ is known after reading only the first five bits ($S$, $D$, $R$). This is the
property that gives takums a decoder whose front end is common to all widths~\cite{hunhold2025avx}.
The characteristic is $c = 2^r-1+C$ for $D=1$ and $c=-2^{r+1}+1+C$ for $D=0$, and the
mantissa is $1.f$ with $f=M/2^p$. In T8 the characteristic may not fit in the
remaining bits; it is then truncated and completed with zeros. The all-zero pattern is the
only zero and $10\ldots0$ is the only NaR (not-a-real); there are no infinities and no NaN payloads.

The decoder is organised in two layers: (i)~field extraction (S, D, R, C, M and the derived $r$, $c$, $p$),
and (ii)~an internal triple (sign, exponent, 64-bit significand) on which the ALU operates.

\subsection{Arithmetic Unit}
Addition, subtraction, multiplication and division share a single encoder. The product of two
significands is computed exactly in 128 bits; the adder aligns exponents in 128 bits with
62 guard bits plus a sticky bit, and subtraction renormalises by the leading one after cancellation.
Results are rounded to nearest, ties to even, on the bit string $(D\,R\,C\,M)$, which
is what the reference library does, and they saturate: overflow never produces NaR and
underflow never produces zero. NaR propagates through every operation.
Because every operation acts on bit patterns, width conversion (\texttt{vncvt.t.t}) reduces to the
encoder's rounding step. Rounding has a single mode and there are no exception flags;
this is the basis of the CSR analysis in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{The Zvtakum Extension}
Zvtakum adds five instructions (Table~\ref{tab:zvt}) that operate on elements whose width equals
\texttt{SEW}, reusing \texttt{vl}, \texttt{vstart}, the mask bit and register grouping of
the standard vector extension. SEW$=64$ is illegal because the project has no T64.
The instructions are placed in the \emph{custom-1} opcode space (\texttt{0x2b}) with the OP-V field layout, because the
reserved OP-V slots belong to RISC-V International.

\textbf{Operand order.} \texttt{vtsub} computes $vs2-vs1$, like \texttt{vfsub.vv}.

\textbf{Dot product.} \texttt{vtdot.vv} is a reduction: \texttt{vd} is an accumulator (as in \texttt{vmacc}), only
element~0 is read and written, and the instruction requires \texttt{vstart}$=0$. Each product is exact (at most 56
significand bits) and is added to an accumulator with a 64-bit significand, more than twice the 27 fraction bits of T32;
only one rounding to SEW bits happens at the end. We considered two alternatives. A widened destination
(T8$\rightarrow$T16, T16$\rightarrow$T32) is not uniform because T32 would need a T64. An exact accumulator (quire, about
1.2\,kbit) is more faithful but far more expensive in hardware. Our choice is uniform across widths; its cost is that a cancellation of more
than $\approx$64 bits loses the residual, which only happens with extreme dynamic range (random T8 bit patterns).
In strip-mined loops each \texttt{vtdot} stores a partial result rounded to SEW bits, and our experiments model exactly this behaviour.

\subsection{Simulator Integration and Validation}
In Spike, the five instructions are implemented as \texttt{insns/*.h} files that call the project's decoder
through a thin glue unit. They are registered in \texttt{encoding.h}, in the build list and in the
disassembler, so traces show them by name. Validation has three layers:
(i)~decode/encode and $+,-,\times,\div$ against \texttt{libtakum}, exhaustively in T8 and with $12$~M random operations each in T16 and T32 (0 mismatches);
(ii)~\texttt{takum\_dot} against \texttt{\_\_float128} and, bit by bit, against an independent Python model with exact rationals (11{,}643 cases, 0 mismatches);
(iii)~a bare-metal program run in Spike with \texttt{--isa=rv64gv} that executes the five instructions with full and partial \texttt{vl}, masks, NaR, zero,
SEW$=8/16/32$ and register groups, and compares with the expected values (22 instruction tests pass; a negative control with corrupted
expected values fails as it should).

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Quadruple-Precision Oracle}
To evaluate the numerical behavior of the proposed \texttt{Zvtakum} extension across scientific computing and machine learning workloads, we evaluate three benchmarks: (i) composite numerical quadrature rules (\texttt{make integracao}), (ii) Sparse Matrix-Vector Multiplication (SpMV) in Compressed Sparse Row (CSR) format, and (iii) a dense General Matrix-Vector Multiplication (GEMV) kernel. Unlike standard x86-64 \texttt{long double} (80-bit extended format with a 64-bit significand), our ground-truth reference $y_{\text{ref}}$ is computed in IEEE 754 quadruple precision (\texttt{\_\_float128} via \texttt{libquadmath}, providing 113 significand bits).

We compare linear Takum (\texttt{T8}, \texttt{T16}, \texttt{T32}) against IEEE 754~\cite{ieee754} \texttt{FP32} and \texttt{FP16}, as well as Open Compute Project (OCP) 8-bit formats~\cite{ocp_fp8} \texttt{OFP8 E4M3} (normal range up to $\pm 448$) and \texttt{OFP8 E5M2} (range up to $\pm 57344$). Two matrix dimensions are evaluated: $200 \times 200$ ($d = 10\%$, averaging 20 non-zeros per row) to exercise non-power-of-two strip-mining tails ($\texttt{vl} < \texttt{VLEN}/\texttt{SEW}$), and power-of-two dimensions ($256 \times 256$ SpMV with $d = 10\%, 20\%$ and $128 \times 128$ dense GEMV) that align evenly with a 128-bit vector register width ($\texttt{VLEN} = 128$, yielding $\texttt{vl} \in \{16, 8, 4\}$ elements per strip for \texttt{T8}, \texttt{T16}, and \texttt{T32}). To avoid division-by-near-zero distortion in element-wise relative error $\vert{}y_i - y_{\text{ref},i}\vert{} / \vert{}y_{\text{ref},i}\vert{}$, reference outputs satisfying $\vert{}y_{\text{ref},i}\vert{} \le 10^{-12} \max_j \vert{}y_{\text{ref},j}\vert{}$ are filtered out across 15 random seeds.

Prior to vector workload evaluation, the arithmetic core was validated on composite numerical integration (trapezoidal quadrature of smooth transcendental integrands) across increasing subinterval counts $N$. Both \texttt{T16} and \texttt{T32} closely track or surpass their IEEE counterparts before reaching rounding saturation, while \texttt{T8} remains bounded without catastrophic overflow as $N$ grows.

In the narrow regime (Fig.~\ref{fig:spmv_cdf}a), all formats operate within their normal exponent bounds. When paired with \texttt{vtdot.vv}, Takum achieves median relative errors of $7.44 \times 10^{-2}$ (\texttt{T8}), $2.63 \times 10^{-4}$ (\texttt{T16}), and $4.19 \times 10^{-9}$ (\texttt{T32}), improving upon chained \texttt{OFP8 E4M3} ($8.79 \times 10^{-2}$), \texttt{FP16} ($7.31 \times 10^{-4}$), and \texttt{FP32} ($8.68 \times 10^{-8}$).

In the wide dynamic range regime (Fig.~\ref{fig:spmv_cdf}b), the fixed exponent fields of \texttt{OFP8 E4M3}, \texttt{OFP8 E5M2}, and \texttt{FP16} suffer severe overflow and underflow, collapsing to a median relative error of $1.00 \times 10^{0}$ ($100\%$). Conversely, Takum completely avoids overflow and underflow across all bitwidths due to its tapered characteristic ($2^{\pm 255} \approx 10^{\pm 77}$ for \texttt{T16}/\texttt{T32} and $\approx 10^{\pm 72}$ for \texttt{T8}). While \texttt{T8} exhibits a high median relative error of $4.55 \times 10^{-1}$ ($45.5\%$) due to coarse 8-bit mantissa quantization at large exponents, it preserves order-of-magnitude information without saturating, whereas \texttt{T16} ($1.77 \times 10^{-3}$) and \texttt{T32} ($2.81 \times 10^{-8}$) maintain high accuracy.

\subsection{Disentangling Format Gain from Accumulator Gain}
To separate the intrinsic accuracy of the Takum format from the benefit of the wide internal accumulator in \texttt{vtdot.vv}, Figure~\ref{fig:dot_vs_chain} and Table~\ref{tab:gemv_spmv_breakdown} compare three accumulation modes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Chained (\texttt{vtmul.vv} + \texttt{vtadd.vv}):} Element-wise multiplication followed by sequential addition rounded to \texttt{SEW} bits at every step (matching standard un-widened IEEE/OFP8 chaining);
    \item \textbf{Strip-Mined \texttt{vtdot.vv} ($\texttt{VLEN}=128$):} The hardware-realizable RVV execution mode where each strip of $\texttt{vl} = 128/\texttt{SEW}$ elements is accumulated in 64-bit internal precision and rounded back to \texttt{SEW} bits in \texttt{vd[0]} at the end of the strip;
    \item \textbf{Full-Row \texttt{vtdot.vv} (Full Acc):} An idealized upper bound where an entire row dot product is accumulated in 64-bit internal precision before a single final rounding to \texttt{SEW}. In hardware without a dedicated cross-strip accumulator CSR, this bound is only reached when the row non-zero count fits within a single vector strip ($nnz \le \texttt{VLEN}/\texttt{SEW}$).
\end{enumerate}

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:gemv_spmv_breakdown}, in narrow-range chained execution without a wide accumulator, \texttt{OFP8 E4M3} and \texttt{FP16} slightly outperform chained \texttt{T8} and \texttt{T16} on dense 128-element reductions because Takum devotes 5 header bits to $(S, D, R)$ to secure its wide dynamic range, leaving fewer mantissa bits at 8-bit width.

When using \texttt{vtdot.vv}, the $3\times$ to $7\times$ error reduction over chained Takum corresponds to the \textbf{Full-Row} accumulation bound ($9.56 \times 10^{-2}$ in \texttt{T8}, $4.14 \times 10^{-4}$ in \texttt{T16}, and $6.13 \times 10^{-9}$ in \texttt{T32}). However, for \texttt{T8} on the dense $128 \times 128$ GEMV workload (8 strips of $\texttt{vl}=16$), the 8 intermediate roundings to 8-bit precision raise the strip-mined median error to $1.76 \times 10^{-1}$, making its accuracy comparable to chained \texttt{OFP8 E4M3} ($1.81 \times 10^{-1}$) when dynamic range is narrow, while retaining full immunity to overflow when inputs are unscaled.

As detailed in Listing~1 and Table~\ref{tab:isa_comparison}, because standard RVV provides widening fused multiply-accumulate (\texttt{vfwmacc.vv}), the strip-mined loop body itself executes 8 instructions per strip in \texttt{T8} (no shift needed) or 9 instructions in \texttt{T16}/\texttt{T32} for both architectures. However, three structural differences arise:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Elimination of Per-Row Reduction Epilogue:} \texttt{vfwmacc.vv} accumulates lane-wise into a widened vector register group (\texttt{v4}), requiring a 5-instruction post-loop epilogue (\texttt{vfredusum.vs} + \texttt{vfncvt.f.f.w} + \texttt{vsetvli} transitions) at the end of every matrix row. In CSR SpMV, where rows typically contain only 1 to 3 strips ($nnz \approx 20$), eliminating this epilogue reduces total per-row dynamic instructions by $\approx 20\%$ to $28\%$.
    \item \textbf{Removal of Architectural Dependency on \texttt{frm} and \texttt{fflags}:} Standard RISC-V floating-point instructions implicitly depend on the \texttt{frm} rounding-mode register and write five sticky exception bits (\texttt{NV}, \texttt{DZ}, \texttt{OF}, \texttt{UF}, \texttt{NX}) in \texttt{fflags} (\texttt{fcsr}). Because Takum defines a single round-to-nearest-even mode and saturates without exception flags, \texttt{Zvtakum} removes the architectural dependency on \texttt{fcsr}, \texttt{frm}, and \texttt{fflags}. The architectural trade-off is that software loses fine-grained exception diagnostics (such as detecting whether saturation or division by zero occurred via \texttt{fflags}), relying instead on \texttt{NaR} propagation for invalid operations.
    \item \textbf{Vector Register Pressure:} Widening \texttt{FP8}$\to$\texttt{FP16} or \texttt{FP16}$\to$\texttt{FP32} doubles the destination register group multiplier ($\texttt{EMUL} = 2 \times \texttt{LMUL}$) and cannot be applied uniformly to 32-bit elements when a 64-bit format is absent. Encapsulating the 64-bit significand accumulator inside the \texttt{vtdot.vv} execution unit preserves $\texttt{EMUL} = \texttt{LMUL}$ across \texttt{T8}, \texttt{T16}, and \texttt{T32}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations and Scope}
\label{sec:limitations}
Several limitations of our current methodology should be noted. First, our implementation targets a functional ISA simulator (Spike); we do not provide an RTL hardware synthesis, meaning silicon area, critical-path delay, energy consumption, and cycle-accurate pipeline stall reductions from removing \texttt{fcsr} dependencies were not measured physically. Second, the internal 64-bit significand accumulator of \texttt{vtdot.vv} is modeled using host x86-64 80-bit \texttt{long double} arithmetic rather than a full 1.2-kbit exact quire, and \texttt{SEW}=64 (\texttt{T64}) is treated as an illegal configuration. Finally, because \texttt{vtdot.vv} rounds the accumulated sum back to \texttt{SEW} bits in \texttt{vd[0]} at the end of each strip-mined iteration, very long dot products in 8-bit precision (\texttt{T8}) experience intermediate rounding loss across strips unless widened to \texttt{T16} prior to reduction.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented the design, implementation, and evaluation of \texttt{Zvtakum}, a custom RISC-V Vector (\texttt{RVV}) extension supporting 8-, 16-, and 32-bit linear Takum arithmetic. By exploiting Takum's invariant 5-bit prefix (\texttt{S, D, R}), our functional model unifies decoding across \texttt{T8}, \texttt{T16}, and \texttt{T32} inside the Spike simulator, while the fused \texttt{vtdot.vv} instruction with an internal 64-bit significand accumulator mitigates intermediate rounding degradation without doubling vector register group pressure ($\texttt{EMUL} = \texttt{LMUL}$).

Empirical evaluation against a 128-bit quadruple-precision reference (\texttt{\_\_float128}) on numerical quadrature, CSR SpMV, and dense GEMV workloads demonstrated that: (1) under wide dynamic ranges where \texttt{OFP8 E4M3}, \texttt{E5M2}, and \texttt{FP16} collapse to $100\%$ relative error, \texttt{T8}, \texttt{T16}, and \texttt{T32} completely avoid overflow and underflow; and (2) \texttt{vtdot.vv} reduces median relative error by $3\times$ to $7\times$ in full-row accumulation and up to $2.7\times$ in hardware strip-mined execution ($\texttt{VLEN}=128$), outperforming \texttt{FP16} in 16-bit GEMV. Finally, \texttt{Zvtakum} eliminates the 5-instruction per-row reduction epilogue required by widening RVV loops and removes architectural dependencies on the \texttt{frm} and \texttt{fflags} CSRs.
